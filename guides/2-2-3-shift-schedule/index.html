How a 2-2-3 shift schedule works

The 2-2-3 pattern creates a repeating two-week cycle of shorter and longer work blocks. The name is useful, but the exact team, start date, day/night assignment, and shift length still need to match your official roster.

Published and reviewed August 26, 2026

The common 14-day sequence

A typical cycle is: work 2, rest 2, work 3, rest 2, work 2, rest 3. That produces seven workdays and seven rest days over two weeks. Teams can be offset from one another so the workplace remains staffed, and some employers use a different starting point in the same sequence.

Work

Days 3-4

Rest

Work

Days 8-9

Rest

Two rest days before the final work block.

Days 10-11

Work

Two more scheduled shifts.

Days 12-14

Rest

The longer rest block before repeating.

Choose the anchor date carefully

The generator needs one known date that corresponds to day one of the cycle. Do not choose an arbitrary recent workday. If the selected date is actually day five or day ten, every future date will be shifted even though the work/rest pattern still looks plausible.

  1. Find the first day of a confirmed two-shift block.
  2. Compare the following 14 days with the official roster.
  3. Confirm whether the assignment is days, nights, or a separate rotating rule.
  4. Add exceptions only after the base cycle is correct.

Hours, weekends, and pay

A 2-2-3 pattern often moves work across weekdays and weekends. Weekly hours can vary depending on where the employer defines the payroll week. Overtime, differentials, holiday pay, and breaks depend on local law and workplace policy, not on the rotation name.

Do not infer pay rules from the pattern

Record the hours and pay settings your employer actually uses. A repeating schedule can predict dates, but it cannot establish overtime eligibility or payroll calculations.
